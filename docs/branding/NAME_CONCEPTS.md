# Name Concept Catalog

## Purpose

This document is the working naming workspace for the product. It is not supposed to be a dumping ground for every plausible word. Its job is narrower:

1. keep the strongest naming directions visible
2. separate live candidates from crowded or weak ones
3. preserve useful visual territory without pretending every name is still alive
4. make the next naming round more disciplined than the last one

Use [NAME_BRANDING.md](NAME_BRANDING.md) for the governing naming rules. Use this file to explore, compare, trim, and retire concepts.

## How To Use This Catalog

1. Judge names by spoken ease first, not by cleverness.
2. Keep active exploration tight: 4 to 6 live names is useful; 40 is not.
3. Treat public-web, GitHub, and early trademark screens as a filter before emotional attachment sets in.
4. Borrow visual direction from weaker names if the naming itself does not survive.
5. Retire names aggressively once they become generic, crowded, awkward, or off-tone.

## Current Read

The strongest naming territory is still **warm cinematic utility**: names that feel calm, memorable, content-first, and household-appropriate without sounding rustic or cute.

The strongest alternate territory is **clean cinematic shorthand**: names that feel branded and modern, but still say cleanly out loud.

After screening the first twelve names, pressure-testing the least risky survivors more deeply, quick-screening the top of Corrected Rounds C, D, and E, and returning to compounds under stricter rules, there is still no clean public-name lock. The deeper Avren pass materially weakened it, so **Bluehour** is back to being the strongest surviving tonal candidate, but only as a conditional alternate because of real namespace friction. **Gloamline** and **Duskcue** are now the strongest screened compound alternates. **Hollis** and **Tavin** remain conditional alternates only. **Lantern**, **Nocturne**, **Hearthlight**, **Afterglow**, and **Titlerow** remain useful tonal, visual, or product-language references. **Avren**, **Marquee**, **Backlot**, **Halo**, **Loom**, **Verse**, **Beacon**, **Calder**, **Alden**, **Elion**, **Aven**, **Calen**, and **Storywell** should not keep leading public-name work.

The weakest territory so far has been **house compounds and ornate coined words**.

- House compounds keep running into crowding or generic search results.
- Longer coined names kept drifting into fantasy, pharma, or overdesigned startup territory.
- Fixed letter-count targets made the results worse, not better.

## What The First Twelve Screens Actually Show

The naming issue is no longer just candidate selection. The current naming convention itself is producing names that sound good quickly but are already occupied.

Observed pattern:

1. Plain real-word names in media, evening, light, or household territory are usually already taken by software, creator platforms, accessories, or entertainment brands.
2. The warmer and more instantly legible the word is, the more likely it already has strong public-web and app-store occupation.
3. GitHub crowding is especially bad for short clean nouns, which makes technical namespace differentiation weaker even before trademark review.

Approach correction:

1. Stop prioritizing broad dictionary nouns as the default first move.
2. Stop treating "sounds nice out loud" as enough to justify another quick-screen candidate in the same word class.
3. Bias the next round toward less publicly occupied word classes: rarer real words, light transformations, or cleaner coined forms that still speak immediately.
4. Keep warmth and cinematic calm, but reduce direct media-metaphor words and common household nouns.
5. Make namespace distinctiveness an input to generation, not just a filter after the fact.

## What The Next Coined-Name Pass Must Do

The coined-name lane should be reset, not forced.

Requirements for the next pass:

1. Prefer 4 to 7 letters, but allow longer only if the name stays extremely easy to say.
2. Prefer one clear pronunciation.
3. Avoid broad standalone dictionary nouns unless there is a specific distinctiveness reason to test one.
4. Avoid obvious surname signals unless the name is otherwise unusually clean.
5. Prefer plainer transformed forms over highly styled letter-shape constructions.
6. Compound words are allowed again only when they avoid generic structural media or household roots.
7. Avoid decorative soft-syllable chains that sound invented for their own sake.
8. Avoid fantasy, pharma, beauty-brand, and enterprise-software energy.
9. Make the name look clean in lowercase and sentence case.
10. Favor recall after one hearing over uniqueness-for-uniqueness' sake.
11. Prefer names that are likely to survive namespace screening before emotional screening.

Sound profiles worth exploring next:

- compact and clean
- slightly cinematic, but not theatrical
- modern without sounding technical
- warm without sounding rustic
- distinctive enough to avoid exact-word app and creator-platform collisions
- brand-neutral rather than surname-heavy
- plainspoken rather than overtly designed
- compound structures only when they feel editorial and branded rather than structural or descriptive

Sound profiles to avoid next:

- faux-Latin endings that make the name feel ornate
- stacked soft vowels that blur together when spoken
- common media nouns that already read like app names
- common household nouns that already read like consumer brands
- obvious family-name or heritage-brand cues unless the name is exceptionally clean
- overly sleek or designerly forms that sacrifice natural speech rhythm
- generic structural compounds that sound like venues, listings, or UI components
- anything that needs pronunciation coaching
- anything that sounds more like a SaaS tool than a viewing product
